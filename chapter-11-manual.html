What You'll Learn

🎯 Chapter Objectives

After studying this chapter, you will understand the purpose and characteristics of three types of fact tables. Each solves different analytical challenges, and mastering all three is essential for designing effective dimensional models.

📌 The Three Types of Fact Tables

  • Transaction Fact Table Most Common — Tracks individual activities/events that define a business process
  • Periodic Snapshot Fact Table For Status — Periodically samples status measurements like balances or inventory levels
  • Accumulating Snapshot Fact Table For Workflows — Tracks progress of an item through a series of processing steps

Some business processes may require only one kind of fact table, but others will require two or more working together. This chapter arms you with an understanding of the capabilities of each, so you can choose the right design for every situation.

Why This Matters

In real-world data warehouses, using the wrong type of fact table is one of the most common mistakes. It leads to complex queries, poor performance, and frustrated business users. Understanding these three patterns will make you a more effective data modeler.

Transaction Fact Tables

Transaction fact tables are the most common type you'll encounter. They capture details about events or activities — things that happen at a specific point in time. By storing facts and associated dimensional detail, they allow activities to be studied individually and in aggregate.

What is a Transaction?

The word "transaction" has a formality to it and connotes the exchange of goods, services, or money. But in fact, any kind of event can be considered a transaction:

  • Booking an order
  • Shipping a product
  • Making a payment
  • Phone calls from customers
  • Student attendance logging

Although no money passes hands in some cases, these activities can still be modeled as transactions.

Three Key Properties

1. Grain of Transaction Fact Tables

The grain can be declared in two ways:

  • By business artifact: "One row per order line" or "One row per transaction"
  • Dimensionally: "Orders by day, customer, product, and salesperson"

Example: Two Ways to Define Grain

Consider an e-commerce order system. The same fact table can be described differently:

Dimensionally "Sales by date, customer, product, and store" Describes which dimensions make each row unique

2. Transaction Fact Tables Are Sparse

This is crucial: rows are recorded only for activities that take place, not for every possible combination of dimension values.

Example

An orders fact table does NOT record rows each day for every combination of salesperson, product, and customer. That would lead to excessive growth and clutter reports with zero-valued rows. Instead, rows are only recorded when orders actually occur.

3. Transaction Fact Tables Contain Additive Facts

Transaction fact tables normally record fully additive facts — measurements that can be summed across all dimensions.

Example: What Makes a Fact "Fully Additive"?

A fact is fully additive if you can sum it meaningfully across ALL dimensions. Consider this sales data:

DateStoreProductQty SoldRevenue
Feb 1NYCWidget10$100
Feb 1NYCGadget5$75
Feb 1LAWidget8$80
Feb 2NYCWidget12$120

✅ Fully Additive: Both qty_sold and revenue can be summed across any dimension:

-- Sum across TIME: "Total revenue for Feb 1-2" SELECT SUM(revenue) FROM sales; -- Result: $375 ✓ -- Sum across STORE: "Revenue by store" SELECT store, SUM(revenue) FROM sales GROUP BY store; -- NYC: $295, LA: $80 ✓ -- Sum across PRODUCT: "Revenue by product" SELECT product, SUM(revenue) FROM sales GROUP BY product; -- Widget: $300, Gadget: $75 ✓

Handling Non-Additive Facts

Most nonadditive measurements, such as ratios, should be broken down into fully additive components. For example, instead of storing "margin rate" (10%), store the components:

  • margin_dollars — fully additive
  • order_dollars — fully additive

Then compute the ratio after aggregation: You want the ratio of the sums, not the sum of the ratios!

Example: Why NOT to Store Ratios (The Math Problem)

Consider these two orders with their profit margins:

OrderRevenueCostMargin $Margin %
Order A$100$80$2020%
Order B$1,000$900$10010%

❌ WRONG: Sum the margin percentages

-- If you stored margin_pct and summed it: SELECT SUM(margin_pct) FROM orders; -- Result: 30% — This is MEANINGLESS!

✅ CORRECT: Compute ratio from sums

-- Store additive components, compute ratio AFTER summing: SELECT SUM(margin_dollars) / SUM(revenue) * 100 AS overall_margin_pct FROM orders; -- Result: ($120 / $1,100) × 100 = 10.9% — CORRECT weighted average!

📌 The Rule

"Ratio of the sums, not sum of the ratios!"
Always store the numerator and denominator as separate facts, then compute the ratio after aggregation.

🔑 Transaction Fact Table Key Takeaways

  • Captures events/activities at specific points in time

Periodic Snapshot Fact Tables

Sometimes, measuring the effect of a series of transactions is as useful as measuring the transactions themselves. These effects are called status measurements.

The Challenge: Studying Status

Common status measurements include:

  • Account balances — the cumulative effect of deposits and withdrawals
  • Inventory levels — deliveries minus removals
  • Water level in a reservoir
  • Temperature readings

Why Transaction Tables Fall Short

Computing balance from transaction history can be inefficient:

  • An account open for 50 years might require aggregating thousands of transactions
  • To get total deposits for all customers, you'd have to do this for every account
  • Some measurements (like temperature) have no corresponding transactions at all

Don't Store Balance with Each Transaction!

You might think: "Why not just add a balance column to the transaction fact table?" This approach has two critical problems:

📌 Problems with Storing Balance in Transaction Facts

  • Problem 1: Sparsity — If there's no activity on a day, there's no row to record the balance. Finding the balance would require searching backward through time.
  • Problem 2: Double-counting — If there are multiple transactions on the same day, the balance would appear multiple times, causing queries to double-count.

Example: Problem 1 — Sparsity Issue in Detail

Imagine you add a running_balance column to your transaction fact table. Here's what the data would look like for Account #12345:

Day Transaction Type Amount Running Balance
2/1Initial Deposit$2,000.00$2,000.00
2/2Withdrawal($20.00)$1,980.00
2/3Check($35.50)$1,944.50
❌ No rows for 2/4, 2/5 — no transactions occurred!
2/6Check($75.00)$1,869.50

🤔 The Problem: A business user asks: "What was the account balance on February 4th?"

-- This simple query FAILS because there's no row for 2/4! SELECT running_balance FROM account_transactions WHERE account_id = 12345 AND transaction_date = '2024-02-04'; -- Result: EMPTY! No rows returned.

😓 The Workaround: To find the balance on 2/4, you must search backward through time to find the most recent transaction:

-- Complex query to find "most recent balance before date" SELECT running_balance FROM account_transactions WHERE account_id = 12345 AND transaction_date = ( SELECT MAX(transaction_date) FROM account_transactions WHERE account_id = 12345 AND transaction_date <= '2024-02-04' ); -- This works but is SLOW and COMPLEX, especially at scale

📊 Scale Problem: Now imagine running this for 100,000 accounts across a year. Each account needs a correlated subquery scanning backward through history. This becomes a major performance bottleneck!

Example: Problem 2 — Double-Counting Issue in Detail

On February 6th, the account had two transactions in a single day. Here's what the data looks like:

Day Transaction Type Amount Running Balance
2/5Deposit$100.00$2,044.50
2/6Check($75.00)$1,969.50
2/6Check($800.00)$1,169.50
2/7Withdrawal($100.00)$1,069.50

🤔 The Problem: A business user asks: "What was the total balance across all accounts on February 6th?"

-- This query produces WRONG results! SELECT transaction_date, SUM(running_balance) AS total_balance FROM account_transactions WHERE transaction_date = '2024-02-06' GROUP BY transaction_date;

❌ What the Query Returns (WRONG):

AccountSum of Balances on 2/6
Account #12345$3,139.00 ❌

The balance was counted TWICE because there were two transactions! ($1,969.50 + $1,169.50 = $3,139.00)

✅ What the Answer Should Be:

AccountActual End-of-Day Balance
Account #12345$1,169.50 ✓

😓 The Workaround: You'd need a complex query to get only the last balance of the day:

-- Must use ROW_NUMBER() or MAX to avoid double-counting WITH ranked AS ( SELECT *, ROW_NUMBER() OVER( PARTITION BY account_id, transaction_date ORDER BY transaction_time DESC ) AS rn FROM account_transactions WHERE transaction_date = '2024-02-06' ) SELECT SUM(running_balance) FROM ranked WHERE rn = 1; -- This works but is complex and error-prone!

The Core Issue

These problems arise because transactions are sparse and can have multiple rows per day, while balances need one definitive value per day. You're trying to store two fundamentally different data patterns in the same table — it doesn't work!

Golden Rule

Storing a transaction and its effect (balance) in the same row of a fact table is always problematic. When you need to study status measurements, use a separate snapshot fact table.

✅ The Solution: Separate Tables for Different Purposes

📝 Transaction Fact Table

Stores individual events

  • One row per transaction
  • Only when activity occurs (sparse)
  • Transaction amounts only

📊 Snapshot Fact Table

Stores daily balances

  • One row per account per day
  • Every day (dense)
  • Balance values only

The Snapshot Model

A periodic snapshot fact table samples the measurement in question at a predetermined interval. This makes it easy to study status without aggregating long chains of transaction history.

Semi-Additivity

This is one of the most important concepts to understand about snapshots:

Semi-Additive Facts Cannot Be Summed Across Time

If you have $100 in your account at the end of each day of the week, that does NOT mean you have $700 at the end of the week! Your account balance is not additive across time.

However, the balance IS additive across other dimensions — you CAN add together balances from multiple accounts on the same day.

Example: Understanding Semi-Additivity with Data

Consider this daily account balance snapshot for the first week of February:

DayAccount AAccount BAccount CDay Total
Feb 1$1,000$2,000$500$3,500 ✓
Feb 2$1,000$2,100$500$3,600 ✓
Feb 3$900$2,100$600$3,600 ✓
Feb 4$900$2,050$600$3,550 ✓
Feb 5$1,100$2,050$550$3,700 ✓
SUM$4,900 ❌$10,300 ❌$2,750 ❌$17,950 ❌
✅ SUM Across Accounts (Same Day)

On Feb 1, the bank has $3,500 in total deposits across all three accounts. This is meaningful and correct.

❌ SUM Across Time

Account A's "total" of $4,900 is meaningless. The account never had $4,900 — it just had ~$1,000 each day.

-- ✅ CORRECT: Sum across accounts for a single day SELECT snapshot_date, SUM(balance) AS total_deposits FROM account_snapshot WHERE snapshot_date = '2024-02-01' GROUP BY snapshot_date; -- Result: Feb 1, $3,500 ✓ -- ❌ WRONG: Sum across time for a single account SELECT account_id, SUM(balance) AS total_balance FROM account_snapshot WHERE account_id = 'A' GROUP BY account_id; -- Result: $4,900 — BUT THIS IS MEANINGLESS!

While semi-additive facts can't be summed across time, they can be meaningfully summarized using:

  • Average — Average daily balance
  • Minimum — Lowest balance in period
  • Maximum — Highest balance in period
  • Last — Period-end balance

Example: Valid Aggregations for Semi-Additive Facts

Using the same data, here are meaningful ways to aggregate across time:

-- Average daily balance for Account A SELECT account_id, AVG(balance) AS avg_daily_balance FROM account_snapshot WHERE account_id = 'A' AND snapshot_date BETWEEN '2024-02-01' AND '2024-02-05' GROUP BY account_id; -- Result: $980 average balance ✓ -- Period-end balance (last day of period) SELECT account_id, balance AS period_end_balance FROM account_snapshot WHERE account_id = 'A' AND snapshot_date = '2024-02-05'; -- Result: $1,100 ✓ -- Minimum and maximum in period SELECT account_id, MIN(balance) AS min_balance, MAX(balance) AS max_balance FROM account_snapshot WHERE account_id = 'A' GROUP BY account_id; -- Result: min=$900, max=$1,100 ✓

Example: The "Sum of Daily Balances" Trick — Computing Average Balance

This is one of the cleverest techniques in dimensional modeling. The challenge: How do you compute average balance across multiple accounts when you can't SUM balances?

The Problem: You want "average daily balance for all checking accounts in February."

AccountFeb 1Feb 2Feb 3Feb 4Feb 5
Acct A$1,000$1,000$900$900$1,100
Acct B$2,000$2,100$2,100$2,050$2,050

❌ The Naive Approach (WRONG):

-- Can't just AVG all balances — this double-counts days! SELECT AVG(balance) FROM daily_snapshot; -- Returns $1,520 (average of 10 rows) -- But this treats Acct A and Acct B as having equal weight...

✅ The Solution: Store sum_of_daily_balances in the monthly snapshot

AccountMonthBegin BalEnd BalSum Daily BalDays in Period
Acct AFeb$1,000$1,100$4,9005
Acct BFeb$2,000$2,050$10,3005

Why it works: sum_of_daily_balances IS fully additive! You can SUM it across accounts, then divide by total days to get a proper weighted average:

-- Correct calculation for average balance across accounts SELECT SUM(sum_of_daily_balances) / SUM(days_in_period) AS avg_balance FROM monthly_snapshot WHERE month = '2024-02'; -- Calculation: ($4,900 + $10,300) / (5 + 5) = $15,200 / 10 = $1,520 -- Now this is the TRUE weighted average daily balance! ✓

Why This Technique is Powerful

This works because:

  • sum_of_daily_balances = $1,000 + $1,000 + $900 + $900 + $1,100 = $4,900 for Acct A
  • You can SUM this value across accounts (it's additive!)
  • Dividing by total days gives you the weighted average
  • Accounts with more days contribute proportionally more to the average

Example: Complete Enhanced Monthly Snapshot

Here's what a fully embellished monthly account snapshot looks like with real data:

Period-to-Date Measurements

A snapshot is the logical home for period-to-date measurements (MTD, QTD, YTD, lifetime-to-date). Like balances, these:

  • Measure cumulative effect of activities
  • Are NOT additive across time
  • Cannot be stored reliably with transactions

Snapshots and Slow Changes

Important Rule for Type 2 Changes

A periodic snapshot records only one row for each combination of natural keys in the dimensions that define its grain. When a dimension undergoes a Type 2 slow change (creating multiple rows for the same entity), the snapshot should only reference the version that was current at snapshot time. Recording more than one would cause double-counting!

🔑 Snapshot Fact Table Key Takeaways

  • Samples status measurements at predetermined intervals
  • Grain = period + what is being sampled
  • Dense — rows exist for every period, regardless of activity
  • Contains semi-additive facts (can't sum across time)
  • Can be embellished with period-begin, period-end, and additive summary facts
  • Only one row per natural key combination (to avoid double-counting)

Accumulating Snapshot Fact Tables

The third type of fact table is used when you need to track the progress of an item through a series of processing steps and analyze the elapsed time between events.

The Challenge: Studying Elapsed Time

Many business processes can be described as a series of stages or steps:

Submitted → Reviewed → Processed → Underwritten → Settled

Common examples include:

  • Manufacturing: ordered → manufactured → QA'd → packaged → shipped
  • Mortgage processing: submitted → reviewed → processed → underwritten → settled
  • Technical support: logged → assigned → diagnosed → closed

The efficiency of a process is often measured by how long it takes to complete steps:

  • Average days between order and shipment
  • Time from application to settlement, grouped by loan type
  • How long tickets spend in each status

Where Transaction Tables Fall Short

You could track status changes in a transaction fact table (one row per status change), but this creates problems:

The Correlated Subquery Problem

To figure out how long it took to review an application requires computing elapsed days between submission and approval — but these are in separate rows. For a group of applications, this requires a correlated subquery — complex to write and poor performing.

Example: The Correlated Subquery Nightmare

Consider a mortgage application process tracked as transactions. Each status change creates a row:

App IDStatusStatus Date
A001SubmittedFeb 1
A001ReviewedFeb 10
A001ProcessedFeb 18
A002SubmittedFeb 3
A002ReviewedFeb 8
A002ProcessedFeb 20
A003SubmittedFeb 5
A003ReviewedFeb 15

🤔 Business Question: "What was the average days from submission to processing completion?"

-- This requires a CORRELATED SUBQUERY — complex and SLOW! SELECT t1.application_id, DATEDIFF( (SELECT status_date FROM status_changes t2 WHERE t2.application_id = t1.application_id AND t2.status = 'Processed'), t1.status_date ) AS days_to_process FROM status_changes t1 WHERE t1.status = 'Submitted'; -- For EACH row in the outer query, the database must: -- 1. Execute the inner query -- 2. Find the matching "Processed" row -- 3. Calculate the date difference -- This is O(n²) complexity! 💀

😓 The Performance Problem:

  • 100 applications → 100 inner queries
  • 10,000 applications → 10,000 inner queries
  • 1,000,000 applications → 1,000,000 inner queries (database meltdown!)

✅ With Accumulating Snapshot:

-- Simple, FAST query with accumulating snapshot! SELECT application_id, days_reviewing + days_processing AS total_days, AVG(days_reviewing + days_processing) OVER() AS avg_days FROM mortgage_processing_snapshot WHERE status = 'Processed'; -- Single table scan, no subqueries, O(n) complexity ✓

What About Begin and End Dates?

Adding begin/end dates for each status helps for adjacent stages, but:

  • Doesn't help when processes aren't linear (items can go back to prior stages)
  • Doesn't help when measuring time across multiple stages (e.g., submission to settlement)

Example: Why Begin/End Dates Don't Solve Multi-Stage Analysis

Even if you add begin_date and end_date to each status row:

App IDStatusBegin DateEnd DateDays
A001SubmittedFeb 1Feb 109
A001ReviewedFeb 10Feb 188

A001SettledFeb 25Mar 14

🤔 Business Question: "Average time from submission to settlement?"

❌ Still requires joining/summing across multiple rows!

-- Still need complex aggregation across rows SELECT application_id, SUM(days_in_status) AS total_days FROM status_history GROUP BY application_id; -- But what if you only want Submitted→Processed (skipping Settled)? -- Now you need WHERE clauses to filter stages... -- And what about applications that went BACK to prior stages?

✅ With Accumulating Snapshot: All stage durations are pre-calculated columns!

-- Direct access to any stage combination SELECT application_id, days_submitted_to_settled, -- Total time days_reviewing + days_processing, -- Just review+processing days_pending_settlement -- Just final stage FROM mortgage_processing_snapshot;

  • Multiple date keys — one for each major milestone
  • Elapsed time facts — days spent at each stage

Life Cycle of a Row

Unlike other fact tables, accumulating snapshot rows are updated regularly. Here's how a single row evolves:

Day 1 — Application Submitted

Row inserted with day_key_submitted = today. Other date keys = 0 (special "not yet" value). days_reviewing = 0.

Day 2 — No Status Change

days_reviewing incremented to 1. Application still under review.

Days 3-9 — Still Under Review

days_reviewing continues incrementing daily.

Day 10 — Review Complete

day_key_reviewed = today. days_reviewing = 9 (final). review_amount = $90,000 (officer changed amount). Now days_processing starts incrementing.

Day 11+ — Processing Stage

days_processing increments daily until next milestone...

"Lags" Between Milestones

The elapsed time facts are sometimes called "lags" because they represent elapsed time between milestones. days_reviewing could also be named lag_submitted_reviewed. Naming after the stage is more flexible for nonlinear processes.

Pairing Transaction and Accumulating Snapshots

Transaction and accumulating snapshot models complement each other beautifully:

Transaction Star Accumulating Snapshot
Study processing volumes at each step Study elapsed time between steps
Analyze workload by stage Measure process efficiency
One row per status change One row per entity (updated)

Best Practice

If you build both, design the accumulating snapshot to use the transaction star as its source. Both can connect to the operational system, but using the transaction star as the source simplifies ETL and eliminates redundancy.

U1: Forwarded to underwriter→PROCESSED (Underwriting stage)

Slow Changes in Accumulating Snapshots

Critical Rule for Type 2 Changes

The accumulating snapshot should contain only one row per natural key of the defining dimension. When a Type 2 change occurs:

  • The dimension table will have multiple rows (old and new version)
  • The fact table should be updated with the most recent surrogate key
  • Never use natural keys in accumulating snapshots — this would cause double-counting

Side Effect to Expect

Because of this, the dimension table may have more rows than the fact table. This is correct and expected! It can pose tuning challenges — star-join optimization may not be the most efficient approach for queries on accumulating snapshots.

🔑 Accumulating Snapshot Key Takeaways

  • Tracks progress through processing steps
  • Grain = one row per identifiable entity (application, order, ticket)
  • Rows are regularly UPDATED — unique among fact tables
  • Multiple milestone date keys — one per processing stage
  • Elapsed time facts track days at each stage
  • Pairs well with transaction stars — each answers different questions
  • Focus on key business milestones, not all operational statuses
  • For Type 2 changes: update to most recent surrogate key

When to Use Each Type

Use Transaction Fact Table When...

  • You need to track individual events or activities
  • You want to aggregate activities by various dimensions
  • You need granular drill-down capability
  • Questions are about "how many" and "how much"

🏪 Real-World Scenario: Retail Sales Analysis

Business Need: Analyze sales patterns, identify bestsellers, track promotional effectiveness

Sample Questions:

  • "How many units of Product X did we sell last month?"
  • "What's the breakdown of sales by store region?"
  • "Which cashier had the highest sales volume on Black Friday?"
  • "Show me all transactions for customer #12345"

Why Transaction Fact Table: Each sale is a discrete event. You need to slice and dice by multiple dimensions (product, store, time, customer, promotion) and drill down to individual receipts.

-- Typical transaction fact query SELECT d.region, p.category, SUM(f.quantity) AS units_sold, SUM(f.sales_amount) AS revenue FROM sales_facts f JOIN dim_store d ON f.store_key = d.store_key JOIN dim_product p ON f.product_key = p.product_key WHERE f.sale_date BETWEEN '2024-11-01' AND '2024-11-30' GROUP BY d.region, p.category;

Use Periodic Snapshot When...

  • You need to track status measurements (balances, levels, inventory)
  • Computing status from transactions would be inefficient
  • Status has no corresponding transactions (temperature, etc.)
  • Questions are about "what was the balance/level at point X"

📦 Real-World Scenario: Inventory Management

Business Need: Track inventory levels, identify stockouts, optimize reorder points

Sample Questions:

  • "What was our inventory level for Widget X at each warehouse on December 31st?"
  • "Show average monthly inventory for all SKUs in Q4"
  • "Which products fell below safety stock levels last week?"
  • "What's our inventory turnover rate by category?"

Why Periodic Snapshot: Inventory level is a status measurement — you need to know the level at specific points in time, not just when things change. Computing level from every shipment and sale would be extremely slow.

-- Typical periodic snapshot query SELECT p.product_name, w.warehouse_name, s.quantity_on_hand, s.quantity_on_order, p.reorder_point, CASE WHEN s.quantity_on_hand < p.reorder_point THEN 'REORDER' ELSE 'OK' END AS status FROM inventory_snapshot s JOIN dim_product p ON s.product_key = p.product_key JOIN dim_warehouse w ON s.warehouse_key = w.warehouse_key WHERE s.snapshot_date = '2024-12-31';

Use Accumulating Snapshot When...

  • Items move through defined processing stages
  • You need to measure elapsed time between stages
  • Individual items can be uniquely identified
  • Questions are about "how long does it take" and "where are delays"

🎫 Real-World Scenario: Customer Support Ticket System

Business Need: Track ticket resolution times, identify bottlenecks, measure SLA compliance

Sample Questions:

  • "What's the average time from ticket creation to resolution?"
  • "How long do tickets spend waiting for customer response?"
  • "Which support tier has the longest resolution times?"
  • "What percentage of tickets meet our 4-hour SLA?"

Why Accumulating Snapshot: Each ticket moves through stages (Created → Assigned → In Progress → Awaiting Customer → Resolved). You need to track elapsed time at each stage and measure end-to-end process efficiency.

-- Typical accumulating snapshot query SELECT t.priority, AVG(s.hours_to_first_response) AS avg_response_time, AVG(s.hours_in_progress) AS avg_work_time, AVG(s.hours_awaiting_customer) AS avg_wait_time, AVG(s.hours_created_to_resolved) AS avg_total_time, SUM(CASE WHEN s.hours_to_first_response <= 4 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) * 100.0 / COUNT(*) AS sla_pct FROM ticket_snapshot s JOIN dim_ticket_type t ON s.ticket_type_key = t.ticket_type_key WHERE s.date_resolved IS NOT NULL GROUP BY t.priority;

Remember: You Can Combine Them!

It's not an either/or decision. A single business process may need two or even all three types of fact tables working together. The inventory process, for example, can benefit from a transaction star (track shipments), a periodic snapshot (track levels), and an accumulating snapshot (track order fulfillment stages).

Chapter Summary

🎓 What You've Learned

This chapter introduced the three fundamental types of fact tables in dimensional modeling. Each serves a distinct analytical purpose, and mastering all three is essential for effective data warehouse design.

Key Points to Remember

📌 Transaction Fact Tables

  • Track events — grain corresponds to individual transaction or summary
  • Sparse — rows only for actual activities
  • Contain fully additive facts
  • Most common fact table type

📌 Periodic Snapshot Fact Tables

  • Sample status at predefined intervals across specified dimensions
  • Dense — unlike transaction tables
  • Contain semi-additive facts (can't sum across time)
  • Can be embellished with period-begin, period-end, and additive summary facts
  • One row per combination of natural keys (avoid double-counting)

📌 Accumulating Snapshot Fact Tables

  • Track progress through processing steps — one row per entity
  • Rows are repeatedly updated as process unfolds
  • Multiple day_keys represent milestone achievements
  • Facts record elapsed time at each stage
  • For Type 2 changes: use most recent surrogate key

Final Advice from Your Senior Architect

🏆 Remember these principles:

  1. Know your analytical requirements first. The right design depends on what questions the business needs to answer.
  2. Don't force one type to do another's job. If you find yourself writing complex queries with correlated subqueries, you probably need a different fact table type.
  3. Multiple fact tables can work together. Transaction and accumulating snapshots complement each other beautifully — don't be afraid to use both.
  4. Never store balances with transactions. This is one of the most common mistakes — it always leads to problems.
  5. Be careful with Type 2 changes. Always use the most recent surrogate key to avoid double-counting.
