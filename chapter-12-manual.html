Factless Fact Tables

Understanding when and how to design fact tables that contain no facts — a powerful technique for tracking events and conditions in your data warehouse.

👋 From Your Senior Architect: Don't let the name confuse you — "factless fact tables" are incredibly useful! This chapter will teach you how to measure business processes even when there are no traditional numeric measurements. Master this, and you'll have a powerful tool in your dimensional modeling toolkit.

What You'll Learn

🎯 Chapter Objectives

This chapter introduces a design technique that might seem paradoxical at first — a fact table with no facts! Despite its name, a factless fact table is incredibly useful for measuring certain business processes. By the end of this chapter, you'll know when to use them and how to design them.

The Paradox

"Factless Fact Table"

A fact table is named for the measurements it stores. So what happens when there are no measurements? You get an oxymoron — but also a powerful design technique!

In a dimensional design, the fact table is where process measurements are stored. These measurements are called facts — that's where the name comes from. Paradoxically, a fact table does not always require facts to measure a process.

📌 Two Types of Factless Fact Tables

  • Factless Fact Tables for Events Events — Record the occurrence of activities. Although no facts are stored, events can be counted to produce meaningful measurements.
  • Factless Fact Tables for Conditions Conditions — Capture important relationships between dimensions that are not part of business activities. Used to study eligibility, coverage, or assignments.

Real-World Examples

Events without facts:

  • Number of documents processed or approved
  • Calls to a customer support center
  • Impressions of an advertisement
  • Attendance records

Conditions to capture:

  • Eligibility of people for programs
  • Assignment of salesreps to customers
  • Active marketing programs for a product
  • Special weather conditions in effect

Why This Matters

Many business processes don't have obvious numeric measurements. Without factless fact tables, you'd be stuck trying to force these into inappropriate designs. This technique gives you the flexibility to model reality as it actually exists.

Events with No Facts

Sometimes you need to track a business activity, but there appear to be no facts to measure. You can define the process, identify the fact table grain, and specify dimensions — but you can't find any numeric measurements.

Don't Panic!

This shouldn't stop you from designing a star schema. When the only measurement is the occurrence of events, you can design a factless fact table. Analysis is conducted by counting rows. The mere existence of a row constitutes a measurement.

Nothing to Measure?

For many business processes, the only measurement is the occurrence of events or activities. There are no dollar amounts to aggregate, no quantities to sum, no balances to average. Yet activities are happening — sometimes at a breakneck pace!

Examples of Countable Events

  • Document Processing: Contracts or applications are received, reviewed, rejected, etc. — measured by counting how many are handled by day, by activity type, by customer, by person doing the processing.
  • Customer Support: Service requests opened, closed, or processed — measured by representative, customer, product, and problem ticket.
  • Advertising: Count the number of impressions (exposures to an advertisement) across various dimensions.
  • Web Analytics: Page views or interface clicks.
  • Attendance: Schools and businesses track presence or absence.

You might hear dimensional modelers say "no measurements exist" for these processes. This is not entirely true! Counts are legitimate and important measurements. It just happens that you don't need to store a fact to support counting.

Key Insight

The fact table may be factless, but the process IS measured. Counting is a valid form of measurement!

The Factless Fact Table Design

Like any other fact table, a factless fact table has a grain. The grain is defined such that each row represents an individual activity. Every row contains:

  • Foreign keys that refer to the dimensional context of the activity
  • Perhaps one or more degenerate dimensions
  • No facts — the presence of the row IS the measurement

Example: Customer Contact Tracking

Suppose a business wants to measure customer contact to fine-tune activities and avoid contacting customers too frequently or infrequently. Contact happens through e-mail and telephone, can be inbound or outbound, and some outbound contacts may be automated (like newsletters).

Using a Factless Fact Table

Although no facts are stored, the factless fact table supports measurement by counting rows. Dimensions can filter or group the counts in useful ways.

Example Query: Count Customer Contacts

How many times was John Smith contacted during January 2009?

SELECT COUNT( contact_facts.contact_type_key ) /* Count any column */ FROM day, customer, contact_facts WHERE customer.name = 'John Smith' AND day.month = 'January' AND day.year = 2009 AND day.day_key = contact_facts.day_key AND customer.customer_key = contact_facts.customer_key

This query returns a single number — the total contacts with John Smith during the month. You can enhance it by grouping:

SELECT contact_type.contact_channel, COUNT( contact_facts.contact_type_key ) FROM day, customer, contact_type, contact_facts WHERE /* ... filters ... */ GROUP BY contact_type.contact_channel

This shows contacts broken down by "E-mail" and "Telephone". Adding the direction attribute would further group by "Inbound" and "Outbound".

Key Point

The events in a factless fact table can be aggregated by counting rows. Any column in the fact table can serve as the basis of the count — they'll all produce the same result.

🔑 Factless Fact Tables for Events — Key Takeaways

  • Activities with no associated facts can be tracked in a factless fact table
  • Each row is a set of foreign keys describing the dimensionality of an event
  • The presence of a row constitutes a measurement
  • Count rows to measure the process — dimensions filter and group results
  • Optionally add a fact with constant value 1 to simplify queries

Conditions, Coverage, or Eligibility

Beyond tracking events, factless fact tables serve another important purpose: capturing conditions. These are relationships between dimensions that exist at a point in time, independent of any particular business activity.

What is a "Condition"?

A condition describes a state of affairs or relationship that exists over time. Unlike events (which happen instantaneously), conditions have duration — they're in effect from one point in time until another.

Examples of Conditions

  • Salesrep-Customer Assignment: Which salespeople are assigned to which customers?
  • Marketing Promotions: Which products are currently part of which promotions?
  • Program Eligibility: Which people are eligible for which programs?
  • Resource Availability: Which facilities are available for use?

Example: Salesrep-Customer Assignments

Consider tracking which salespeople are assigned to which customers. This isn't a transaction — it's a condition that exists over time. A row represents the assignment itself, not an event.

Why Not Use a Bridge Table?

You might wonder why we don't just use a many-to-many relationship. The answer is that we want to track when assignments are in effect, not just that they exist. The date dimensions let us query "Who was assigned to this customer in Q1 2009?"

Comparing Conditions with Events

The real power of factless fact tables for conditions becomes apparent when you compare conditions with events. This reveals insights that neither alone could provide.

Business Questions That Require Both

  • Which salesreps took orders from customers they weren't assigned to?
  • Which assigned salesrep/customer pairs had no orders this quarter?
  • How do promotion-eligible products compare to actual sales?
  • Which eligible members didn't use their benefits?

Important Consideration

Comparing conditions with events requires careful attention to time ranges. An assignment that was in effect during any portion of the period should be included. This can make queries complex — we'll cover techniques in the next section.

🔑 Factless Fact Tables for Conditions — Key Takeaways

  • Conditions capture relationships that exist over time, not instantaneous events
  • Use effective/expiration date dimensions to track duration
  • The real value comes from comparing conditions with events
  • This comparison reveals gaps, overlaps, and exceptions
  • Also called "coverage tables" — they show what's covered/eligible/assigned

Correlating Events and Conditions

Now let's explore how to actually query the comparison between conditions and events. This is where things get interesting — and a bit complex!

Three Approaches

📌 Ways to Compare Events and Conditions

  • Set Operations (UNION, INTERSECT, MINUS) — Use SQL set operations to combine or compare results from two queries.
  • Correlated Subqueries — Use EXISTS or NOT EXISTS to find matching or non-matching records.
  • Precompute the Results — Build a derived star that contains the comparison, making queries simple.

Using Set Operations

SQL set operations can compare results from two separate queries. To find assignments without orders (Region B from our Venn diagram), use MINUS:

-- Query 1: Get all salesrep/customer assignments in Q1 2009 SELECT assigned_rep.salesperson_name, assigned_customer.customer_name FROM salesrep ALIAS assigned_rep, customer ALIAS assigned_customer, day ALIAS effective_day, day ALIAS expiration_day, customer_assignment_facts WHERE effective_day.date <= '3/31/2009' AND expiration_day.date >= '1/1/2009' AND ...joins... MINUS -- Query 2: Get all salesrep/customer orders in Q1 2009 SELECT order_rep.salesperson_name, order_customer.customer_name FROM salesrep ALIAS order_rep, customer ALIAS order_customer, day ALIAS order_day, order_facts WHERE order_day.date >= '1/1/2009' AND order_day.date <= '3/31/2009' AND ...joins...

Understanding MINUS

MINUS (called EXCEPT in some databases) returns rows from the first query that don't appear in the second. So this gives us:

All assigned pairs − Assigned pairs with orders = Assigned pairs without orders

You can also use INTERSECT to find assignments with orders, or reverse the MINUS to find orders from non-assigned pairs.

Pro Tip: Numbering Time Periods

Date range conditions like "effective during any part of Q1" can be tricky. Some designers add a quarter_number attribute to the date dimension (1, 2, 3, ...) so conditions become simpler:

-- Instead of complex date math: effective_day.quarter_num <= 15 AND expiration_day.quarter_num >= 15

Using Correlated Subqueries

Another approach uses NOT EXISTS or EXISTS with a correlated subquery. This can be more flexible when you need additional information from one of the tables.

-- Find assignments with no orders using correlated subquery SELECT assigned_rep.salesperson_name, assigned_customer.customer_name FROM salesrep ALIAS assigned_rep, customer ALIAS assigned_customer, day ALIAS effective_day, day ALIAS expiration_day, customer_assignment_facts WHERE effective_day.date <= '3/31/2009' AND expiration_day.date >= '1/1/2009' AND ...joins... AND NOT EXISTS ( -- Subquery: Look for orders SELECT * FROM salesrep ALIAS order_rep, customer ALIAS order_customer, day ALIAS order_day, order_facts WHERE order_day.date >= '1/1/2009' AND order_day.date <= '3/31/2009' AND ...joins... AND -- Correlation: Match natural keys order_customer.customer_id = assigned_customer.customer_id AND order_rep.salesrep_id = assigned_rep.salesrep_id )

Use Natural Keys for Correlation!

The correlation should use natural keys, not surrogate keys. Why? If either entity has undergone a Type 2 slow change, there may be multiple surrogate keys for the same real-world entity. Natural keys ensure you're matching the same customer/salesrep regardless of dimensional changes.

Precompute the Results

The set operations and correlated subqueries are complex. They're hard to write, hard to read, and may perform poorly on large datasets. Sometimes the best solution is to compute the results in advance.

Build a Derived Star

Create a new star schema that holds the pre-computed comparison. For "assignments without orders," this derived star would have:

  • A period dimension (the summarized period)
  • A salesperson dimension
  • A customer dimension
  • Optionally: facts like total assignment days, potential revenue, etc.

This approach moves complexity to ETL time, making queries trivially simple. It's an example of a derived schema (covered in detail in Chapter 14).

When to Precompute

  • When queries are too complex for end users
  • When BI tools can't generate the required SQL
  • When performance is unacceptable
  • When the comparison is frequently needed

Slowly Changing Dimensions and Conditions

When dimensions in a condition star undergo Type 2 slow changes, these changes will require new fact table rows.

Example: Customer Name Change

Suppose Lawrence Falconi is assigned to ABC Holdings. Later, ABC Holdings changes its name to ABC International (a Type 2 change creates a new dimension row). The new version must also be associated with Lawrence:

effective_day expiration_day salesrep_key customer_key Notes
1001 1089 45 (Falconi) 789 (ABC Holdings) Original assignment
1090 9999 45 (Falconi) 790 (ABC International) New row for Type 2 change

Complications for Queries

Type 2 changes complicate comparison queries. If names are used for matching, an old assignment name won't match a new order name. Always use natural keys in correlation, not names or surrogate keys that might change.

🔑 Correlating Events and Conditions — Key Takeaways

  • Set Operations: Use MINUS/EXCEPT, INTERSECT, UNION to compare query results
  • Correlated Subqueries: Use EXISTS/NOT EXISTS for more flexibility
  • Precomputed Results: Build a derived star for simple queries and good performance
  • Always use natural keys for correlation to handle Type 2 changes correctly
  • Type 2 changes in condition dimensions require new fact table rows

Chapter Summary

🎓 What You've Learned

Factless fact tables may have a paradoxical name, but they're a powerful and essential technique. You now know when to use them and how to design them for both events and conditions.

Key Points to Remember

📌 Factless Fact Tables for Events

  • Some processes are measured only by counting events or activities
  • These can be modeled with a fact table whose grain is a single activity — no facts required
  • Analysis is conducted by counting fact table rows
  • Dimensions filter, group, sort, and drive subtotals
  • A fact with constant value 1 can be added to simplify SQL

📌 Factless Fact Tables for Conditions

  • Some important relationships don't correspond to a business activity
  • Examples: eligibility, coverage, assignments, promotions
  • Conditions are modeled using a factless fact table with effective/expiration dates
  • The real power comes from comparing conditions with activities

📌 Correlating Events and Conditions

  • Use set operations (MINUS, INTERSECT) for simple comparisons
  • Use correlated subqueries when you need extra flexibility
  • Precompute results in a derived star for simplicity and performance
  • Always use natural keys to handle Type 2 slow changes correctly
  • Type 2 changes generate new rows in condition fact tables

Final Advice from Your Senior Architect

🏆 Remember these principles:

  1. Don't be fooled by "no measurements." If you can count events, you have a measurement. Design the factless fact table!
  2. Conditions are relationships, not events. They capture "what was true" rather than "what happened."
  3. The magic is in the comparison. Conditions alone are useful, but comparing them with events reveals the most valuable insights.
  4. Consider your users. If queries are too complex, precompute the results into a derived star.
  5. Natural keys are your friend. They survive Type 2 changes and make correlations reliable.
